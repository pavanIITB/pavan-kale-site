One working unit tells you that the design can work. A small batch tells you how much the result depends on a particular part, a specific person assembling it, or a workaround that nobody wrote down.

Give the next few units the same acceptance test. Pay attention to which steps take longer than expected and which measurements vary across units. If three people assemble them, compare where their process differs. A better assembly instruction is sometimes the cheapest way to remove variation. Other times you’ll find a part that needs a clearer tolerance or a jig that makes the correct position obvious.

Make a short record of failures and how each was caught. A defect caught before packing is a different problem from one a customer finds after a week. The small batch pays for itself when it tells you where the next repeatability problem will show up.

## Leave yourself a useful record

A small batch is a good time to label units. Which one was assembled with which parts, and who ran the test? A cheap label or a row in a spreadsheet can help you make sense of a failure later. Without it, you can end up taking apart every unit to find out which one used a component that had changed.

Ask what you can make visible before the next batch. Sometimes that means adding a check after a difficult assembly step. Sometimes it means updating a drawing so the correct orientation is clear. Give the next batch one or two improvements you can observe. Spread-out changes make it harder to know which improvement made the process more reliable.
