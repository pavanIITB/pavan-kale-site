Ask someone to walk you through the first time they used the product. Give them room to remember the details. What did they do first? When did they pause? Did they go back to the instructions?

Look for the moments that come up in more than one conversation. A feature request is a useful starting point. The story behind it can reveal smaller changes you can try first, such as a clearer instruction or a control in a more obvious place.

It helps to write down observations separately from your interpretation. “Looked for the control on the side of the bottle” is an observation. “The button is in the wrong place” is a hypothesis. The distinction matters when you compare interviews, because the same awkward action could have more than one cause. A short follow-up with the next customer may tell you which cause is worth testing.

## Follow up on the pattern

Once you have a couple of conversations, choose one friction point to explore in the next one. If three people struggled with setup, ask the fourth person to describe their first minute with the product. Their account might confirm the pattern or point at a different cause. Writing down the details of each situation makes the comparison easier.

When you follow up with someone, show the smallest change that responds to what you heard. A revised line in the instructions or a clearer indication on the product can be enough to test the idea. It also gives the person a chance to say the change is helping with a different problem from the one they originally described.
