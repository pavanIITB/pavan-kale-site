A working prototype usually starts at the point where its maker already knows what to do. A customer starts with a box, a set of expectations, and their own routine.

Watch someone open the box and go through setup. Check what happens when they return to the product a few days later, after they’ve forgotten the instructions. Write down anything they have to look up or ask about. Those moments are part of the product and can be tested with the same care as the hardware.

Run the test with the same packaging, instructions, and accessories a real customer would get. If a simple setup step fails, it helps to know whether the problem started in the instructions, on the product, or during unpacking. Repeat the test after changing just that part. You’ll learn more than you would by redesigning every touchpoint at once.

## Include the moments people recover

A person’s first week rarely follows the exact sequence in an instruction sheet. They might be interrupted halfway through setup, leave the product alone for several days, or lend it to somebody who hasn’t seen the instructions. Those are ordinary situations to include in a test. They often show whether the product gives someone enough feedback to figure out what state it’s in.

When you find a sticking point, write down what the person tried first. Their choice gives you a clue about what the product is already communicating. An improvement might be as small as putting a status indication where someone actually looks or giving the same name to an action in both the instructions and the interface.
